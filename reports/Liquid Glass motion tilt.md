# Apple promises motion-aware glass, Flutter must derive tilt itself

Apple confirms that Liquid Glass lighting "in some cases" responds to device motion, but publishes no sensor, API, angle range, damping or frame rate, and no reverse-engineering writeup exists ([WWDC25 219](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/)). The only concrete observation is hands-on: app-icon lighting shifts as the phone tilts, from a default upper-left light ([Apple Magazine](https://applemagazine.com/liquid-glass-ios-26-the-optical-illusion-making-some-iphone-screens-tilt/)). The Flutter and web recreations are weaker than they look. The one Flutter package with a motion hook feeds gyroscope angular velocity into a light angle as if it were an angle, so its highlight jumps while the phone turns and snaps back when it stops. For Hearthrise, the best fit for the existing idle-drift `GlassTilt` is the low-passed accelerometer gravity vector from `sensors_plus` (one stream, no integration drift). Turn gravity into roll and pitch relative to a slowly tracked neutral pose. Apply a deadzone and a soft clamp. Smooth per frame with a dt-based exponential. Blend the result with idle drift through an eased weight. Hold a static neutral under Reduce Motion. Stop everything when the app or tab is not visible. Everything numeric below the "verified" line is tuning judgement and must be checked on a real device, because neither simulator delivers useful sensor data.

## What Apple actually says, and what it leaves out

The single primary statement is from the WWDC25 "Meet Liquid Glass" transcript: "in some cases, the lighting responds to device motion, making it feel like Liquid Glass is aware of its position in the real world" ([WWDC25 219](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/)). The transcript never uses the words tilt, gyroscope or specular. The word "specular" and the idea of reacting to "movement" come from Apple's press release, which says the material "dynamically reacts to movement with specular highlights" ([Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2025/06/apple-introduces-a-delightful-and-elegant-new-software-design/), quoted in the earlier Hearthrise design-spec report, not re-fetched). Apple separately describes lights that "move in space" on system interactions such as unlocking, which is an event-driven animation, not a sensor-driven one ([WWDC25 219](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/)).

Hands-on reporting narrows the scope. One outlet says app-icon lighting "shifts as users tilt their iPhones, recalculating reflections in real time", with a highlight on one edge and deeper shadow on the opposite edge ([Apple Magazine](https://applemagazine.com/liquid-glass-ios-26-the-optical-illusion-making-some-iphone-screens-tilt/)). Another reports that the shifting highlights are "less noticeable in motion than in static screenshots" ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/liquid-glass-vertigo-3598657/), via search summary because the page returned 403). Which other components tilt-react (tab bar, buttons, sheets) is unverified. Whether Low Power Mode disables the effect rests on one unreadable page and is unconfirmed. Touch response is documented in words only: glass "instantly flex[es] and energiz[es] with light" and the glow spreads from the fingertip to nearby glass ([WWDC25 219](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/)). That is separate from tilt.

Reduce Motion matters most for design. Apple says it "decreases the intensity of some effects and disables any elastic properties" ([WWDC25 219](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/)). Whether it freezes the tilt highlight specifically is not stated. Some users reported dizziness and eye strain with Liquid Glass, mostly on dark wallpapers ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/phones/ios/ios-26-users-say-liquid-glass-is-causing-eye-strain-and-vertigo-here-are-the-possible-fixes)); the claim that tilt in particular drives it is one article's argument, not evidence. Taken together, these reports support a restrained design. The effect should be a small, slow shift of the rim light around a fixed upper-left baseline (an inference consistent with the reports, not documented), and it should switch off for users who ask for less motion.

## Third-party recreations copy the idea but not the maths

The Flutter ecosystem has one motion hook and several tilt-card packages. `liquid_glass_widgets` 1.13.0 ships `GlassMotionScope`, which subscribes to a `Stream<double>` of radians, calls `setState` on every event, and overrides the theme `lightAngle`, with no smoothing, clamping or rate limiting ([source](https://github.com/sdegenaar/liquid_glass_widgets/blob/main/lib/widgets/shared/glass_motion_scope.dart)). Its documented example maps `gyroscopeEvents.map((e) => e.y * 0.5)` ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sdegenaar/liquid_glass_widgets/main/README.md)). Because `sensors_plus` defines gyroscope values as rotation rate in rad/s ([gyroscope_event.dart](https://github.com/fluttercommunity/plus_plugins/blob/main/packages/sensors_plus/sensors_plus_platform_interface/lib/src/gyroscope_event.dart)), that example treats a velocity as an angle (my reading of the units; the package does not say so). Its default light angle is `0.75 * pi`, described as upper-left, matching iOS 26. `liquid_glass_renderer` has no sensor code at all. Its pub line 0.2.0-dev.4 has a configurable `lightAngle`, but the unreleased 1.0 rewrite removes it and fixes the light at the top ([CHANGELOG](https://github.com/whynotmake-it/flutter_liquid_glass/blob/main/packages/liquid_glass_renderer/CHANGELOG.md)). If the app ever moves to that line, tilt has to be a custom highlight painted above the glass.

The tilt-card packages show practical plumbing. `holographic_card_flutter` smooths each event with an exponential retain factor of 0.85, then lerps the displayed offset 0.1 per 16 ms tick, but also feeds angular velocity straight in as an offset and calls `setState` every frame ([repo](https://github.com/SirAntonySir/holographic_card_flutter)). `flutter_tilt` 4.1.0 decouples sensor rate from render rate by storing the latest sample and pushing it from a periodic timer. It reads the accelerometer separately to infer device orientation, and defaults to `sensorRevertFactor = 0.05` and `sensorMoveDuration = 50 ms` ([repo](https://github.com/fluttercandies/flutter_tilt)). None of the surveyed Flutter or web code implements a deadzone or a complementary filter.

On the web, the recipe is well established. Feature-detect `DeviceOrientationEvent.requestPermission`, call it from a click inside a secure context, then listen to `deviceorientation`, reading `gamma` (left-right, -90 to 90) and `beta` (front-back, -180 to 180) ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/DeviceOrientationEvent/requestPermission_static), [W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/2024/WD-orientation-event-20240504/)). `vanilla-tilt.js` calibrates a zero point by averaging the first 10 events and maps a -45 to +45 degree window to element travel with a 15 degree maximum tilt ([source](https://github.com/micku7zu/vanilla-tilt.js/blob/master/src/vanilla-tilt.js)). `parallax.js` defaults to friction 0.1 and calibrates the Y axis against the initial reading ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wagerfield/parallax/master/README.md)). No public tilt-driven liquid-glass web demo was found; the SVG and CSS glass libraries surveyed do not use `deviceorientation`. Two lessons carry over to Flutter: calibrate against a neutral instead of absolute angles, and sample the sensor and render on separate clocks.

## Accelerometer gravity beats gyro for a decorative tilt light

`sensors_plus` 7.1.1 supports Android, iOS and partial Web only, not desktop, and needs Flutter 3.19 or newer ([pub.dev](https://pub.dev/packages/sensors_plus)). The current API is `accelerometerEventStream({samplingPeriod})`; the older `accelerometerEvents` getters are deprecated ([source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fluttercommunity/plus_plugins/main/packages/sensors_plus/sensors_plus/lib/sensors_plus.dart)). The accelerometer stream includes gravity, in m/s², while the gyroscope reports rotation rate ([pub.dev](https://pub.dev/packages/sensors_plus)). `SensorInterval` values are 200 ms for `normalInterval`, about 66.7 ms for `uiInterval`, 20 ms for `gameInterval` and zero for `fastestInterval` ([sensor_interval.dart](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fluttercommunity/plus_plugins/main/packages/sensors_plus/sensors_plus_platform_interface/lib/src/sensor_interval.dart)). On Android the delivered rate is a hint and not guaranteed, and Android 12 and later cap motion sensors at 200 Hz unless `HIGH_SAMPLING_RATE_SENSORS` is declared ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fluttercommunity/plus_plugins/main/packages/sensors_plus/sensors_plus/README.md), [Android docs](https://developer.android.com/develop/sensors-and-location/sensors/sensors_overview)). Requesting 50 Hz is far below that cap, so no extra permission is needed. On iOS, since 6.0.0 the README requires `NSMotionUsageDescription` in Info.plist and warns that omitting it crashes the app when it reads motion data. Web ignores the sampling period.

My recommendation is gravity from the accelerometer, with the gyroscope skipped. This is an inference from the sensor properties, not a measured comparison. Integrating gyro rate drifts, correcting it needs a complementary filter, and `sensors_plus` does not expose the platform's fused orientation. A highlight that follows how the phone is held needs only a slowly varying absolute angle, which gravity provides with one stream. The cost is that shaking contaminates the gravity vector, handled below.

### Tilt math, from gravity to a normalised offset (inference; unit-test with known poses)

Normalise the sample to a unit vector (gx, gy, gz) and discard it if the raw magnitude falls outside roughly 7 to 12.5 m/s², since shaking or free-fall makes it meaningless. In portrait, flat face-up reads about (0, 0, +9.8) and upright reads about (0, +9.8, 0). Compute `roll = atan2(gx, sqrt(gy² + gz²))` for left-right tilt and `pitch = atan2(gy, gz)` for forward-back tilt, which is well conditioned in the normal 30 to 60 degree hold. Do not hard-code the hold angle. Track a neutral (rollN, pitchN), compute `dRoll = wrap(roll - rollN)` and `dPitch = wrap(pitch - pitchN)`, and normalise with `t = clamp(d / range, -1, 1)`, using a full-deflection range of 20 to 25 degrees. That is tighter than vanilla-tilt's 45 degree window and consistent with blog guidance of 20 to 30 degrees for handheld use. iOS and Android sign conventions in `sensors_plus` are unverified, so test both. Landscape needs axis swapping; the simplest option is to hold idle drift whenever the app is not in its primary orientation.

### Recentring and the flat-on-table trap

A fixed neutral fails because people hold phones at different angles. Let the neutral chase the current pose with a very long time constant (about 8 to 15 s), so tilt behaves as a high-pass signal, and reset it on resume from background. The failure case is a phone lying on a desk. If the neutral was learned in hand, flat reads as a large pitch offset. Detect flat (`|gz| > 0.95`, within about 18 degrees of horizontal), face-down and upside-down; in those states fade tilt weight to zero, fall back to idle drift and freeze neutral updates.

## Smoothing, reduce-motion and lifecycle decide whether it feels right

None of the following constants comes from a cited source; they are tuning starting points. Filter in two stages. Stage one low-passes gravity at sensor rate with a time constant of about 0.1 to 0.2 s. Compute alpha from event timestamps as `1 - exp(-dt / tau)` instead of hard-coding it, because delivery rates vary. Stage two runs in a `Ticker` at display rate, easing the displayed value toward the target with tau of about 0.25 to 0.4 s, with dt clamped to at most 0.1 s after a pause. This second stage also removes the steppiness of a 15 Hz source, so `uiInterval` or `gameInterval` is enough. Apply a deadzone of 1 to 2 degrees as subtract-and-rescale, not a hard cut, and a soft limit such as `tanh` near the clamp so the light does not hit a wall. Keep amplitude small (a few percent of widget size) and let depth come from slow response. Blend with the existing drift as `out = lerp(idle(t), tilt, w)`, where `w` eases between 0 and 1 over roughly 0.6 to 1.0 s. Force `w` to 0 when no event has arrived for over 1 s, the sensor errors or does not exist, the phone is flat, or motion is reduced. A slow Lissajous such as `(sin(0.13t) * 0.35, cos(0.09t) * 0.25)` is a reasonable drift shape, though the existing drift should stay as it is.

For accessibility, Flutter exposes two separate flags. `MediaQueryData.disableAnimations` reflects Android's "Remove animations" and asks that animations be disabled or reduced ([API](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/widgets/MediaQueryData/disableAnimations.html)). iOS Reduce Motion is exposed separately as `AccessibilityFeatures.reduceMotion`, which is iOS-only and describes "parallax effects removed" ([API](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/dart-ui/AccessibilityFeatures/reduceMotion.html)). One forum report says `disableAnimations` returned false on iOS with Reduce Motion on ([itsallwidgets](https://forum.itsallwidgets.com/t/should-flutter-honor-ioss-reduce-motion-option-in-accessibility-settings/4657)); that is older and single-source, so verify on device. Because tilt is parallax, treat either flag as a reason to output a static neutral and not subscribe to sensors. A `MediaQueryData.reduceMotion` commit is dated 2026-07-31 and its release is unconfirmed, so read `platformDispatcher.accessibilityFeatures.reduceMotion` directly and re-check on `didChangeAccessibilityFeatures`. Also offer a persisted "Motion effects" toggle.

For lifecycle, Android docs say to always unregister listeners, that sensors are not disabled when the screen turns off, and that background apps on API 28 and later receive no continuous-sensor events ([Android docs](https://developer.android.com/develop/sensors-and-location/sensors/sensors_overview)). Cancel the subscription on `inactive`, `paused` and `hidden` via `WidgetsBindingObserver`, resubscribe on `resumed` and reset the neutral. Stop the sensor and the `Ticker` when the tilt consumer is not visible (route covered, tab unselected). Always pass `onError` to the stream, since low-end devices lack sensors ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fluttercommunity/plus_plugins/main/packages/sensors_plus/sensors_plus/README.md)). Quantified battery cost of 15 versus 50 Hz was not found; Android only advises choosing the largest delay that works. A 15 Hz accelerometer is likely negligible next to display repaint (inference).

## Performance and a testable architecture fit the existing GlassTilt

The repo already exposes tilt through `GlassTilt` (an `InheritedWidget` holding a notifier that `lib/ui/` widgets read with `GlassTilt.of`) and `atmosphere.dart` eases it along idle drift. The smallest change keeps that contract: put a `TiltController` in `lib/features/` that owns the subscription, filters, `Ticker` and notifier, and swap it in where the idle-drift value is produced. `lib/ui/` stays stateless, which matches the project rules. This is a design inference from the file layout. Write the tests first: inject a `Stream<AccelerometerEvent>` through a small `TiltSource` interface, drive it with a `StreamController`, pass a fixed dt, and assert known poses (flat, upright, 20 degrees left, shaking, silent stream, reduce-motion on). The simulator point matters: the iOS Simulator has no Core Motion data (unverified general knowledge) and a guide warns that motion effects render incorrectly there ([Atelier Socle](https://www.atelier-socle.com/en/articles/swiftui-liquid-glass-guide), weak search-summary source), so device feel is an explicit manual check.

For rendering, Flutter's guidance is to localise rebuilds, pass static subtrees as `child`, and keep `build()` cheap ([best practices](https://docs.flutter.dev/perf/best-practices)). `BackdropFilter` is "relatively expensive", should be bounded with `ClipRect`, and `BackdropFilter.grouped` can merge filters ([API](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/widgets/BackdropFilter-class.html)). The practical rule, an inference, is to notify only when the value moves by more than about 0.002 and drive a `CustomPainter(repaint: notifier)` so each tick repaints only the highlight. Wrap the highlight in a `RepaintBoundary` and keep it above the blur. A moving light under a backdrop region would force the blur to re-run every frame. Stop the `Ticker` once the value settles and no drift is needed, such as under reduced motion. Profile on a device and check repaint rainbow and `saveLayer` counts; no per-device `BackdropFilter` costs were found.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing from "reproduce Apple's tilt" to "build a plausible, restrained one". Apple's own claim is qualitative and limited, and the best-known Flutter motion hook is physically wrong, so there is no reference implementation to match. The defensible bar is a drift-free, deliberately small rim-light shift that degrades gracefully to the idle drift you already have.

Three open items need a real device before the constants are trusted: the iOS and Android axis signs, the actual delivered sample rate per `SensorInterval` (read it from event timestamps), and whether `disableAnimations` or `reduceMotion` fires on current iOS. Also decide early whether glass stays on `liquid_glass_renderer` 0.2.x, where `lightAngle` is drivable, or moves to the 1.0 line, where it is not and tilt needs a custom overlay.
